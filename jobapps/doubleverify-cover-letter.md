Dear DoubleVerify Product Design Team,

The line in the role description that did it for me was "define clear, consistent interaction patterns for both human users and agentic AI workflows" — and then the tool callouts for Cursor AI, Claude Code, and Claude Design sealed it. Most listings drop AI into one bullet of the qualifications and move on. This one frames AI as something the design team is actively shaping the language of, not just adopting. That's the conversation I've been quietly having with myself, and through every project I've taken on, for about two years now.

The shortest version of my AI-research thread: as a research assistant at Indiana University Indianapolis, I spent a long stretch using NotebookLM and Gemini to apply Conceptual Metaphor Theory across 5,000+ Reddit posts — basically a stress test of how well AI can do qualitative coding against a non-trivial theoretical framework. The interesting result wasn't "AI does qual coding." It was the confidence-band reliability: high-confidence interpretations consistently matched expert human judgment; medium-confidence outputs read like a trained student researcher; low-confidence ones were nonsense more often than not. That gave me a working heuristic for when to lean on AI versus when to step back and do the work myself — and I find myself reaching for that heuristic every time I touch a design problem with AI somewhere inside it.

A couple of those design problems, briefly. Twenty moderated usability sessions with older adults on an AI-driven health information tool — work that surfaced concrete patterns around trust, privacy, and the very specific transparency moves that decide whether people actually act on what the AI tells them. And validation work on an AI-assisted energy dashboard for manufacturing teams, including field research at the Smart Manufacturing Roadshow in Indiana, where the AI chatbot's explainability ended up being the load-bearing design problem rather than the dashboard itself. Different industries, same recurring question: how do people calibrate trust in AI when the stakes are real? Which is the question I think any team designing for agentic workflows has to answer well.

I gravitate toward systems thinking too. I designed a smart cane and companion app for managing POTS — a chronic condition that causes sudden heart rate spikes — as one integrated system across a watch, a phone, and physical cane hardware, with the cane's heart-rate sensor and the watch's interface both feeding the same real-time monitoring flow. The episode-warning screen was the hardest part: the user might be dizzy, brain-fogged, or close to fainting, so I had to design for legibility, large touch targets, and a calming tone all at once. That instinct — break a multi-device problem into clean interaction patterns and don't hide the complexity from the user — is the one I bring to every system I touch.

Data visualization isn't where I've spent the most time, but it's close enough to matter. The energy dashboard was a real-time data product — voltage, current, frequency, machine state — where the design problem was making complex data legible without burying the user. And as a research assistant, I supported field studies of an interactive sustainability data viz at the Indiana State Museum, using Kinect-based motion capture to track how children physically engaged with it. Both are angles on the same underlying question: how do you turn data into something a non-specialist can actually use?

Where I've landed on AI-assisted design tools, honestly: the biggest win isn't raw speed. It's that AI lets you fail faster, iterate faster, hit the next milestone faster, and start mapping a sector of the industry before anyone else does. It accelerates how quickly you converge on the right version of a product. The less-obvious win — the one I think about more — is that streamlining your own workflow with these tools surfaces the gaps in that workflow: where automation actually does the work, where it just looks like it does, and where you still need human presence, human context, and real research to land on user needs that hold up. My portfolio is a small case study of that. I built it on Cursor and Google Antigravity, deliberately experimenting across the full model stack — Gemini 3.1 Pro High, Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet, and the lighter tiers — at very different intensities. Partly to ship things fast (the interactive 3D viewers for each project, for example, alongside a browser-based AR virtual try-on for eyewear in React, Three.js, and MediaPipe). Partly to map out where each model actually earns its place in a real design workflow. The case studies live at fernandojluna.com.

A bit of background, since I should be straight about where I am. I'm finishing my Master's in Human-Computer Interaction at Indiana University Indianapolis, focused on accessibility in UX research. I'm also currently serving as Lead Product Designer on a capstone project with TruePulse Health — an early-stage company collecting physiological data from a sensitive user group (women considering future pregnancy, women currently pregnant, and women in postpartum) at an IU research lab using a Polar H10 chest-strap sensor. The system I'm designing has to be sensor-agnostic so the same protocol can run on whatever hardware actually ends up in the field; simple enough for a participant who may be under physical or emotional stress to put on, pair, and complete the protocol independently; and clean enough on the researcher side for stakeholders to retrieve that data locally or in the cloud. We had to design the participant flow with the awareness that this user group's relationship to the body, to wearables, and to data being collected on them is not neutral — the protocol can't feel clinical or intrusive, and the brand had to land as supportive rather than performative. Before that capstone, I served as Lead Product Researcher at Darzy.ai, a remote-first startup, working directly alongside the founder and product designer.
