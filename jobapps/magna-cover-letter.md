Dear Magna New Mobility Team,

The line in the Group Summary that grabbed me was "turning our hardware into smart, integrated platforms." That hardware-software boundary — designing UX that lives across physical devices, sensors, and software in one connected experience — is where most of my recent work has been pointing, and it's the part of UX I find most interesting because the design decisions have to defend themselves against both interaction logic and real physical constraints.

The clearest example is a research-driven project I led: a smart cane and companion app for managing POTS, a chronic condition causing sudden heart rate spikes, designed across a watch, a phone, and physical cane hardware as one integrated system. The cane handle housed a heart rate sensor for passive monitoring, an integrated SOS button, and a medication pill box; the app handled real-time episode detection, guided breathing exercises, and symptom logging that auto-populated from the connected hardware. The hardest design problem was the episode-warning screen — the user might be dizzy or close to fainting, so I had to design for legibility, large touch targets, and an emotionally calming tone all at once. Different domain, but the same instinct as mobility UX: the system has to be honest about physical state and let the user act fast without overloading them.

More recently, I served as Lead Product Designer on a capstone project with TruePose Health, an early-stage company collecting physiological data from a sensitive population — women considering future pregnancy, women currently pregnant, and women in postpartum — at an IU research lab using a Polar H10 chest-strap sensor. The company was effectively greenfield, so the work covered the full lifecycle: drafting the research protocol, selecting methods, building out the research plan, and shaping a brand identity and product purpose the team could actually carry forward. The core design problem was the system itself — sensor-agnostic so the same protocol could run on whatever hardware ended up in the field, simple enough for participants to put on, pair, and complete a data-collection protocol on their own, and clean enough for researchers and other stakeholders to retrieve that data locally or in the cloud. Same hardware-software boundary as POTS, with the added weight of designing for participants under real physical and emotional stress and for a stakeholder group that doesn't usually share tools with end users.

Closer to the New Mobility group's industrial side, I led validation work on an AI-assisted energy dashboard for manufacturing teams, including field research at the Smart Manufacturing Roadshow in Indiana with real users — emerging IT workers, deans of engineering, and senior operations managers with 30+ years on the floor. The work surfaced trust, transparency, and visual hierarchy issues in both the dashboard and the AI chatbot that high task-completion rates were quietly hiding, and translated those into specific design recommendations. That's the closest analog I have to industrial intralogistics or fleet operations UX — role-aware, operationally heavy surfaces where small inefficiencies compound across thousands of hours of work.

I also ship my own front-end. A recent solo project — a browser-based AR virtual try-on for eyewear — went from Figma into React, Three.js, and MediaPipe, which kept me honest about which interactions feel great in a static frame but actually fall apart at 30 FPS on a mid-range phone. That hands-on engineering exposure makes me a faster collaborator with software and embedded teams on what's realistically buildable versus what looks clever in a mockup. The full case study is at fernandojluna.com.
