Dear Google XR Team,

The line in the role description that did it for me is the XR team's framing of what's next — "lightweight XR devices like smart glasses and headsets pair with helpful AI to augment human intelligence, offering personalized, conversational, and contextually aware experiences." That convergence of AI and XR isn't a direction I'd be learning on the job. It's the exact intersection my last two years of work have already lived at.

The most relevant project in my portfolio for this role is a browser-based augmented reality virtual try-on for eyewear that I designed and built end-to-end — Figma into React, Three.js, and MediaPipe. It runs a 9-screen user journey and a 5-state interaction pattern (IDLE → PERMISSION → FIND_FACE → LOCK → TRACKING → LOST), with face tracking driving live, registered rendering and a recovery state for when tracking is lost. Building it meant doing the AR research and the AR build in the same hands: competitive analysis of how Warby Parker, Ray-Ban, and Zenni handle the same problem, then the production pipeline that ships it. That's the closest thing in my work to the role's "research in AR/VR product environments" ask — an immersive product where whose experience gets centered is decided in the interaction architecture, not after it.

The preferred qualifications around emerging devices and human physiology are where the rest of my work clusters. I built Wave & Watch, a gesture-controlled smartwatch prototype in Python, OpenCV, and MediaPipe using 21-keypoint hand tracking, originally as an accessibility research piece for users with motor disabilities — which made human performance and ergonomics the actual design constraint, not a footnote. I designed a smart cane and companion app for managing POTS, a chronic condition causing sudden heart rate spikes, as one integrated system across a watch, a phone, and physical hardware. And I'm currently leading my graduate capstone as Lead Product Designer with TruePulse Health, an early-stage company collecting physiological data from women across the pregnancy continuum at an Indiana University research lab using a Polar H10 chest-strap sensor. Wearables, body data, and visual processing aren't adjacent interests for me; they're what I've been building research around.

On research methods and the AI half of the XR mission: I've spent two-plus years as a Research Assistant across two HCI labs at Indiana University Indianapolis, leading workstreams under each of my MS HCI co-directors. In Dr. Francesco Cafaro's lab I ran field studies at the Indiana State Museum using Kinect-based motion capture and recruited across very different cohorts. In Dr. Aquesha Martin-Hammond's lab I led an AI-assisted qualitative analysis study applying Conceptual Metaphor Theory across 5,000+ Reddit posts, and ran 20 moderated usability sessions with older adults on an AI-driven health information tool. The finding I keep coming back to — that confidence-band reliability matters more than the AI output itself, that high-confidence interpretations matched expert judgment while low-confidence ones were frequently nonsense — is exactly the question a "helpful AI" paired with XR has to answer well: when does it actually augment the person, and when does it just pretend to. Field studies, interviews, usability testing, surveys, and logs-style analysis are working tools for me, and as Lead Product Researcher at Darzy.ai I built a research operation from scratch — recruitment, protocols, synthesis, and a prioritized research direction that fed product decisions — which is the closest analog I have to owning a research roadmap.

One last thing, and it's the part of this I feel more than argue. Google's reach means whatever this team ships lands in the hands of people across an enormous range of abilities, languages, ages, and circumstances — and the posting's own framing, fair-chance hiring language included, reads like a company that takes that breadth seriously. My Master's is focused on accessibility in UX research and design, which is really just the discipline of remembering the people a system wasn't originally built around. I've spent enough of my own life as the person a product wasn't quite designed for to know how much it matters when someone does the research to close that gap. Designing for the edges so the center holds for everyone is the instinct I'd want to bring to XR specifically — the technology is still new enough that whose experience we center is genuinely up for decision.

One honest flag. The role asks for six years in an applied research setting, and I'm two-plus years in across the labs, Darzy.ai, and the TruePulse capstone. What I'd offer against that gap is density: AR, wearables, AI evaluation, accessibility, and physiological-data systems aren't five separate lines on my resume — they're the same two years, because emerging-technology research is what I deliberately built that time around. The preferred advanced-degree qualification fits cleanly.

The practical note: I'd be relocating from Indianapolis, most likely choosing the San Jose location, and a role doing XR research in the room where the devices are being built is exactly the move I'm looking to make next.

I'd love to talk through any of this in more detail. Thanks for considering me.
