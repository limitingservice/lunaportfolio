Dear Startup Lab Team,

The role description names exactly what I've been chasing in my own work for the last two years: "build a new product from the ground up in 3 months." Add the founder-style framing — roles overlap and evolve, wear as many hats as it takes — and this reads less like an internship and more like the kind of compressed, full-stack design problem I actually want to be in the middle of. The pieces I'd usually have to argue for (research-driven product design, end-to-end ownership, bias toward action) are the defaults here. The pieces that aren't yet — new tooling, an unfamiliar domain, a team's working rhythm — are where I tend to learn fast, adjust to feedback in real time, and hold myself accountable for closing the gap rather than just acknowledging it. Good collaborative work, for me, has always meant being the person who can pick up what the team needs and run it without it becoming someone else's problem.

The most direct parallel to this role from my recent work is the capstone I'm currently leading as Lead Product Designer with TruePulse Health — an early-stage company collecting physiological data from a sensitive user group (women considering future pregnancy, women currently pregnant, and women in postpartum) at an IU research lab using a Polar H10 chest-strap sensor. The company was effectively greenfield when I came in, so the work covered the full lifecycle: drafting the research protocol, selecting methods, building out the research plan, shaping a brand identity, and designing a sensor-agnostic system that handles participant onboarding, sensor pairing, protocol-guided data collection, and researcher data retrieval (local or cloud, depending on context). The participant flow had to be designed with the awareness that this user group's relationship to the body, to wearables, and to data being collected on them is not neutral — the protocol couldn't feel clinical or intrusive, and the brand had to land as supportive rather than performative. That's a "we have real users and a real question but the company is still mostly an idea" situation, which is the situation this role describes.

The other direct parallel is my most recent professional role: Lead Product Researcher at Darzy.ai, a remote-first startup, working directly alongside the founder and product designer. I built the research operations from scratch — recruitment pipelines, interview protocols, synthesis templates, shared insight repositories — because the company didn't have one yet, and I led a small team of interns through participant recruitment, user interviews, and analysis. The pattern of "translate user signal into design action without translation overhead, then ship the next iteration" is one I'd recognize quickly here.

A few of the other projects to show the range. I designed a smart cane and companion app for managing POTS — a chronic condition that causes sudden heart rate spikes — as one integrated system across a watch, a phone, and physical cane hardware, with the cane's heart-rate sensor and the watch's interface both feeding the same real-time monitoring flow. The episode-warning screen was the hardest part: the user might be dizzy, brain-fogged, or close to fainting, so I had to design for legibility, large touch targets, and a calming tone all at once. I also ship my own front-end: a recent solo project — a browser-based augmented reality virtual try-on for eyewear — went from Figma into React, Three.js, and MediaPipe, with a 9-screen user journey and a 5-state interaction pattern (camera permission → face acquisition → tracking lock → live try-on → recovery on lost tracking). That "do the design AND the build" range is part of why founder-style environments make sense to me — I've felt the cost of every interaction decision in code, not just in mockups.

On the research-into-design side, the muscle is real. I led a mixed-methods study on Canvas LMS across eight user roles combining interviews, observational task analysis, time-on-task and click-count measurement, and heuristic evaluation, then translated the findings into specific design recommendations. I ran 20 moderated usability sessions with older adults on an AI-driven health information tool. I led validation work on an AI-assisted energy dashboard for manufacturing teams, including field research at the Smart Manufacturing Roadshow in Indiana. The thread through all of it: translate user signal into actionable design moves, then iterate.

Most of that research depth comes out of two-plus years as a Research Assistant across two HCI labs at Indiana University Indianapolis — Dr. Francesco Cafaro's lab (interactive data visualization, museum field studies, embodied cognition) and Dr. Aquesha Martin-Hammond's lab (human-centered AI for older adults). I've contributed across the full research lifecycle in both labs and owned specific workstreams: participant recruitment across very different cohorts (museum professionals, children and family museum visitors, older adults), qualitative coding and thematic analysis, scenario-based task survey design, AI prompt engineering for theory-driven analysis, and synthesis to lab teams. The work is still active: I'm continuing the AI-assisted qualitative analysis thread that started with the 5,000+ Reddit study (refining NotebookLM and Gemini prompts against Conceptual Metaphor Theory), and supporting the field-study deployment of an interactive sustainability data visualization at the Indiana State Museum. It's been my most direct training in operating across disciplines, methods, and stakeholder groups at once — and rotating leadership of different parts of a study without losing the through-line — which feels like exactly the muscle a small, flat, cross-functional team needs.

Where I've landed on AI in the design workflow, since velocity in a 3-month window depends on it: honestly, the biggest win isn't raw speed. It's that AI lets you fail faster, iterate faster, hit the next milestone faster, and explore a sector of the industry before anyone else does. It accelerates how quickly you converge on the right version of a product. The less-obvious win — the one I think about more — is that streamlining your own workflow with these tools surfaces the gaps in that workflow: where automation actually does the work, where it only looks like it does, and where you still need human presence, human context, and real research to land on user needs that hold up. My portfolio site is a small case study of that. I built it on Cursor and Google Antigravity, deliberately experimenting across the full model stack — Gemini 3.1 Pro High, Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet, and the lighter tiers — at very different intensities. Partly to ship features fast (interactive 3D viewers for each project, the AR try-on integration). Partly to map out where each model actually earns its place in a real design workflow. The case studies live at fernandojluna.com.
