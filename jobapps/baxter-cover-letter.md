Dear Baxter Global Design Team,

The line on the Baxter site that grabbed me was "Save and Sustain Lives." It's the kind of mission statement easy to roll past until you've actually designed for someone whose physical state is at stake — and most of my recent design work has been exactly that, which is part of why this role caught my attention.

The clearest example: I designed and prototyped a smart cane and companion app for managing POTS, a chronic condition that causes sudden heart rate spikes, dizziness, and fainting on standing. The hardware concept included a heart rate sensor in the cane handle for passive monitoring, an integrated SOS button, and a built-in pill box; the companion app handled real-time episode detection, guided breathing exercises, and symptom logging that auto-populated from connected devices. The hardest design problem in the project was the episode-warning screen — the user might be dizzy, brain-fogged, or near fainting, so I had to design for legibility, large touch targets, alert-state hierarchy, and a calming tone all at once. That same craft surface — labeling, iconography, and UI state language for a user under stress — is the closest analog I have to Baxter's product labeling and UI work.

More recently, I served as Lead Product Designer on a capstone with TruePose Health, an early-stage company collecting Polar H10 physiological data from a sensitive population (women considering future pregnancy, women currently pregnant, and women in postpartum) at an IU research lab. The company was effectively greenfield, so the work spanned the full lifecycle: research protocol drafting, method selection, brand identity creation, and a sensor-agnostic system that handled participant onboarding, sensor pairing, protocol-guided data collection, and stakeholder data retrieval locally or in the cloud. That "design has to work for the patient, the researcher, and the regulatory chain" instinct is the part of medical product design I gravitate toward.

I also built a gesture-controlled smartwatch prototype (Wave & Watch) in Python, OpenCV, and MediaPipe — using real-time hand tracking to let users with motor disabilities like Parkinson's navigate watch features through finger counting instead of touch. Different surface, but the same instinct as Baxter's portfolio: design for users whose interaction range is constrained, and don't pretend they aren't.

On the tooling side: Figma is my primary design tool, used for multi-screen high-fidelity prototypes, design system components, and the kind of UI concept work this role calls for (POTS, TruePose, and a recent browser-based AR e-commerce project I built end-to-end are all in Figma). I use Adobe Illustrator and Photoshop regularly for graphics, iconography, and infographic work, and Adobe Premiere Pro for video research artifacts. Sketching is part of my early concepting flow. The full case study work is at fernandojluna.com.

I'm finishing my Master's in Human-Computer Interaction at IU Indianapolis, focused on accessibility in UX research, and most recently served as Lead Product Researcher at Darzy.ai, a remote-first startup, working directly alongside the founder and product designer. I'm based in Indianapolis, so Batesville is a short and realistic commute or move — the 3-days-in-office hybrid model is one of the reasons this role lands as practical rather than aspirational.
